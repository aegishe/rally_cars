This is one of the fastest racing cars ever built. When it was created, it was faster than
the Grand Prix cars of the time. It smashed lap records, and it was built on the basis
of a hugely successful Le Mans prototype. This is the story of the Porsche 919 Hybrid Evo.
Porsche had quit the World Endurance Championship at the end of the 2017 season,
though design work was thought to have started on an all-new car for the 2018 WEC,
but instead the manufacturer would not race that season after all, as it was preparing
for its debut season in the all-electric Formula E Championship. However, Porsche's racing
department wasn't idle. I suspect that a conversation was had between some of the
senior management and engineers over a beer or two, and a question was raised,
how fast could the 919 really go, if not constrained by regulations? Without fuel
flow or aerodynamic limitations, without a weight limit or deployment and harvesting limits on the
hybrid system, just how far could that car really go? The 919 was already expected to do a global
PR tour in its standard configuration as a tribute to the car's huge success, but the idea of the
Ultimate 919 was amazingly accepted by the Porsche management, and the engineers were given the all
clear to develop another new version of the car, which would be called the 919 Hybrid Evo.
Now, before we look at the specific development of the Evo, it's worth taking a look at the
919 LMP1 in its post-Le Mans 2017 configuration. The car had actually started the season in its
low drag format, but for the post-Le Mans rounds of the World Endurance Championship that season,
the bodywork was revised with shorter, lower and somewhat smoother and more curvy front wheel
pods with smaller headlight lenses. The dive planes were also changed along with the front
splitter. From the rear, the differences to the bodywork are clear to see, with the trailing
edge of the car quite different to the Le Mans specification. The overall aim of these
changes was simple, to boost the downforce level of the car overall for the lower average speed
of the WEC circuits. That late season car essentially became the basis of the Evo package.
With chassis 4 of the 2017 Porsche 919s, the car which had actually led much of the 2017 Le Mans
24 hours before it retired in the closing stages with an engine failure when it had a lead of
over an hour. And that chassis was the one which was selected specifically for converting into the
new specification car. It had actually only been introduced at the Le Mans test day, so it had
a very low mileage. Aerodynamic development started in CFD, but extensive wind tunnel testing was
also undertaken using Porsche's own Aero Acoustic Wind Tunnel. This aerodynamic program had a very
clear aim to boost the downforce level even further, but without generating too much aerodynamic drag on
the car. However, with no technical regulations to worry about, dramatic changes to the design
were quickly introduced. For example, a completely new nose design was introduced right away,
with a narrower and shorter front impact structure. And with no need for headlights on the car,
these were removed to save weight. But there were no mirrors fitted to the car at all,
which was another weight saving. Again, with no rules enforced, there was no need for the
overwheel air extractors, the big holes used on all Le Mans prototypes, to try to prevent them
from flipping over at high speed, though notably the LMP1's stability fin on the
engine cover remained in place. The overall side pod shape of the 919 was actually quite
similar between the Evo and the 2017 car, as you can see here. At the rear of the car,
there were substantial changes, including a completely new twin element rear wing.
To reduce aerodynamic drag on the straights, the wing featured active aerodynamics, or a drag reduction
system if you want to call it that. The wings would open on the straights and close in the
corners, and for this to work well, Porsche would need completely new wing profiles.
So it went online and found a vast library of possible wing profiles. In fact, Porsche
trialled 1600 different wing profiles, and using what the company called machine learning,
it whittled that number down to the final designs, which were then bolted onto absolutely huge end
plates. Crucially and additionally, the floor of the car was also heavily revised. Not as visible,
or not visible at all, was a movable aerodynamic device on the trailing edge of the front splitter,
or front wing if you look at the car in that sense, it further cut drag on the straights.
Without the need to comply with the regulations, the front splitter was significantly reworked,
and the diffuser was massively enlarged. To say that the 919 EVO was unlimited in its
development isn't quite true, there were some limitations placed on the Porsche team. It had
to use the LMP1 car as the basis of this new machine, not a complete clean sheet design. While
there were some budgetary limits to the car project as well, and this was particularly evident when it
came to the side skirts on the car. Fixed skirts were used on the floor edges, but sliding skirts
were contemplated and even experimented with in the design phase of the 919 EVO,
but the Porsche engineers simply couldn't get the sliding skirts to work without a complete
redesign of the side pod, and there simply wasn't the time or budget for that.
So flexible skirts were then tried out, but these couldn't be made to work reliably,
resulting in a fixed skirt approach. These fixed skirts offered a significant boost
in downforce levels, and did shift the car's centre of pressure forwards somewhat,
but the time and the money just simply wasn't there to get a more advanced skirt solution
working. Overall, the new aerodynamic package of the 919 EVO increased the downforce level by
a massive 53%, and it had an efficiency increase of 66% compared to the qualifying trim used at
Spa in 2017, but it is worth noting that that was the low drag specification of the car and not the
later high downforce version of the car, so that downforce level increase probably isn't quite
as big as the 53% figure suggests. However, the changes to the car were not just aerodynamic,
the mechanical package of the car was also drastically revised, but only revised.
The core package of the car carried over in its most basic form, the 2017 monocoque was used in
identical configuration, and the car still used a 2.0-litre V4 turbocharged engine,
featuring direct injection and an exhaust gas energy recovery system.
The front-mounted MGUK section of the hybrid was also retained, and the transmission and its
absolutely huge casing was also retained, as was the amusingly named suspension concept of the car.
Don't google it. However, many of these elements were massively revised. The engine, for example,
no longer needed to run with a fuel flow restriction, so the fuel flow meters were removed
from the car, which was another weight saving, but it also meant that more fuel could reach
the combustion changes, there was no limit on that, and with that factor removed,
the engine output was also hugely increased. In 2017 LMP1 trim, the V4 put out a bit under 500
brake horsepower, though in the 2015 specification it had a bit more than that. In the EVO specification,
though, it had 720 brake horsepower. The hybrid system linked to the engine was also significantly
boosted. While the LMP1 regulations put limits on how much energy could be harvested and deployed
on each lap, the EVO was not subject to any such limit, and that brought the total power level of the
unit to around 1160 brake horsepower. However, perhaps more performance could actually have been
unlocked with a Formula One-style dedicated race fuel and co-developed combustion chambers,
but that wasn't possible again due to time and budget constraints.
The inboard suspension was also revised slightly to increase roll stiffness, and the front to
rear interconnected suspension system was also revised additionally. There were quite a lot
of weight savings across the car, such as the removal of windscreen wipers, the air jack system,
after all there were no pit stops, and all of the FIA's race control electronics were taken
out of the car as well. But not every change to the chassis reduced the weight, indeed some
critical changes actually increased the weight slightly. For example, with the very high
speeds and the high cornering loads and the higher aerodynamic loads passing through the car,
compared to the standard 919, the car had to be fitted with new stronger wishbones in the suspension,
while the steering system was also strengthened for the same reason, and that slightly increased
the weight of those specific components. The braking system was also heavily revised to cope
with the higher expected speeds, but also it utilised a four-wheel brake by wire system
to give the car a little bit of your control. Overall, compared to the LMP1 specification car,
the 919 EVO was 39kg lighter at 849kg, and actually I'm kind of surprised it wasn't a bit more than
that, but again I suspect that's down to the two big constraints that Porsche faced with this car,
time and money. Notably, Michelin actually developed some special tyres just for this car,
which were designed for short, high-speed runs rather than endurance racing, which further boosted
the mechanical grip level of the car, and that allowed the car to really push lap records.
The final car was shaken down at Porsche's test track at Weissach in Germany,
where it later broke the lap record, and after the initial testing confirmed the car's performance
and reliability, the updated machine was shipped to Spa in Belgium, where it took to the track
and went on to utterly shatter the lap record, beating Lewis Hamilton's 2017 pole position time
by almost 8 tenths of a second. The 919 EVO was officially then faster than a Formula 1 car,
but it wouldn't keep the Spa record for particularly long, as Sebastian Vettel in a
Ferrari went 2 tenths of a second quicker than the 919 EVO had done a few months later during
qualifying for that year's Belgian Grand Prix. Oscar Piastri today holds the outright record at Spa,
set in a McLaren in 2025, and that record is now over a second faster than the Porsche went.
Yet this shouldn't detract from the Porsche's pace. In 2018, the Porsche would still have been
4th fastest in qualifying 2 at the Formula 1 Grand Prix, and in 2025, its 1 minute 41.7 lap
would have put its 7th on the grid for the Belgian Grand Prix. However, one record that the 919 retains
to this day is perhaps its most impressive, the outright Nurburgring Nordschleife record, which
had previously been set by Stefan Beloff at the wheel of a Porsche 956, who covered the almost 13
mile lap in just over 6 minutes, a record which stood right up until the arrival of the 919 EVO,
and when the EVO arrived at the ring, the car set out on its fast laps, and soon,
a completely new record was established almost a minute faster than Beloff had gone in the 956.
At 5 minutes and 19 seconds driven by Timo Bernhardt, the Porsche 919 was the fastest car
to ever lap the Nordschleife, and it remains so to this day. On that run, the car set an
average speed of 233.8 kph, and it hit a top speed of 369.4 kph. And when you start to think about
those speeds, you start to wonder, could the car have gone after Honda's Bonneville 400 record?
I wonder. The car went on to conduct demonstration runs at a number of different events,
and at Brands Hatch in England, the car came very close to the outright lap record on the
Short Indy circuit. This was not meant to be a record attempt, however, and as such,
the car wasn't fitted with the special tyres used at Spa and the Nürburgring,
however, the car did come within 1.1 seconds of the outright record. Notably, the man behind
the wheel, Nick Tandy, had never actually driven the EVO before that run. A technical glitch on
the car actually cut the run short, and as a result, it didn't break the record,
the record which it wasn't officially trying to break. And despite what AI might tell you when
poorly written Google articles, the outright lap record at Brands Hatch was set by a Lola
Cosworth Champ car driven by Paul Tracy in 2003, a 37.006 second lap. And it's important to note
that the lap record is not held by a modified historic F1 car, which set a very quick race lap
a few years later, but it's not the outright lap record on the Brands Hatch Indy circuit.
That run that is quoted by the historic F1 car was over a second slower than the Lola had gone
in 2003. The 919 then found its way to the USA for more promotional work, and at Laguna Seca
during another demonstration run, the car actually lapped the track quicker than Helio Castroneves'
best time in a kart car, but it was still slower than Marc Genet had gone in a Ferrari
demonstration run in 2005. But again, the car wasn't really setting out to go for any records at that
event. There was some talk of the 919 making an attempt at the Pikes Peak hill climb as well,
but the lack of a steel roll cage on the car and the difficulty of fitting it with one,
which was required for that event probably prevented the 919 from doing so, but certainly I think
conversations were had and they may have fed into the later Volkswagen IDR entry at Pikes Peak.
In the end, the 919 Evo's last ever public run was on the Public Road in Germany, where it's
driven from the factory to the Porsche Museum, where the car resides to this day. Indeed,
during its life, the 919s, not just the Evo but all of them, did make quite a few appearances
on the public road, but these were demonstration runs of the racing cars. However, in secret,
Porsche was taking that experience to develop a wild new road car. The 919 Street would utilise
the monocoque of the Race 919, of which quite a number were built, with at least five cars wearing
2017 chassis numbers. The car would also use the 2.0-litre V4 engine, and this was very much
to be an exclusive road car for sale to very wealthy customers. Porsche got as far as developing
all-new bodywork for the car, which is very much a production car product, and it lacked a lot of the
aerodynamic devices of the racing car, for obvious reasons it didn't need them, it was a road car.
But in the end, the 919 Street project was abandoned and it didn't ever make it past
a full-scale clay model. But Porsche's intent was very, very real, and some years later it
actually did create a similar concept of car, based on the LMDH racecar, the 963.
Now you may find online, in a few places, references to the Capricorn 01 Zagato sports car,
being based on the 919 chassis, but that's just not true. A quick look at the chassis of the car
will show you it's nothing like a Le Mans prototype chassis, even though it is quite neat
and was produced by the same organisation which manufactured the 919 Tubbs. It is not though
a road-going 919 derivative, it's a completely separate design, but the true 919 Street would
have been absolutely wild had Porsche ever finished its construction. Overall though,
the Porsche 919 was a truly remarkable machine. It contested 26 races and won 16 of them,
and it finished on the podium in every single race it contested. To this day, it holds the
outright lap record at the Nurburgring and remains one of the fastest racing cars ever built,
and in reality, it probably could have gone even faster than it ever did,
with a bit more budget and a bit more development and a bit more time,
but that wasn't the point of the car. It achieved all it set out to do, there was
nothing left for the 919. Looking back at the 919 or 9R9 as it was known internally,
it could be seen as the last true Porsche racing car. All subsequent competition cars wearing
the German manufacturer's brand on the nose and also using its name have either been production car
based machines like their GT cars or based on the designs of a completely different company.
The LMDH specification Porsche 963 is a multi-matic design which shares its chassis
with the abandoned Audi LMDH project, and multi-matics never seen the LMP2 car.
Meanwhile, the Porsche Formula E car uses a Dolara chassis which is shared by all of the
teams in that series. The 919 though, was a dedicated Porsche racing car,
and to date it was the last of its kind. I do wonder if we will ever see it's like again.
